    GDPR Legal Bases

    Under GDPR Art. 6, no processing is lawful unless it fits one of six named bases: consent, contract, legal obligation, vital interests, public task, or legitimate interests. The requirement is not "have a good reason" — it is to pick one basis per purpose, in advance, and to be able to name it.

    #The mapping in privacy-policy-template

    §5 does exactly that: a purpose column and a basis column, one row per activity.

    Purpose Basis claimed
    Operate, maintain and secure the ServiceLegitimate interests — running a safe, functioning website
    Respond to emails, questions or feedbackConsent / steps taken at your request
    Comply with legal obligations and lawful requestsLegal obligation
    Detect, prevent and address abuse, fraud or security incidentsLegitimate interests

    Three of the six bases used, three unused — contract, vital interests and public task are simply inapplicable to a static personal site and are not listed. The section closes by ruling out advertising, profiling and automated decision-making producing legal or similarly significant effects, which is Art. 22 answered pre-emptively rather than a marketing claim.

    #Why the per-purpose granularity matters

    A basis is not a property of an organisation, it is a property of a processing operation. The same email address is held under consent when you write in and could be held under legal obligation if it were subpoenaed — different bases, different rights attached. That is why §9's rights list is asymmetric: withdrawal of consent only bites on the rows where consent is the basis, and objection only bites on the legitimate-interests rows.

    Legitimate interests is the only basis here carrying a balancing test — the controller's interest against the data subject's rights. It passes easily precisely because so little is collected; see data-minimisation. The two concepts are load-bearing for each other.

    #Beyond Art. 6

    The policy pairs the GDPR mapping with a CCPA/CPRA track that works on a different logic entirely — not "name your basis" but "disclose, and honour opt-outs" — with a no-sale/no-share attestation and a 45-day response window against GDPR's 30. §9.3 extends a catch-all to LGPD and PIPEDA. The supervisory authority is named concretely (Poland's UODO), which is the detail most template policies leave blank.
