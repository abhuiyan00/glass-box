    Sprague-Grundy and the Nim-Sum

    Nim is solved. Not "solved well" — solved exactly, by an XOR.

    The nim-sum of a position is the bitwise XOR of the pile sizes. A position with nim-sum 0 is a P-position: the player about to move loses under perfect play. Any position with nim-sum ≠ 0 is an N-position, and there is always a move making the nim-sum 0. So optimal play is: compute s = p₁ ⊕ p₂ ⊕ … ⊕ pₙ, find a pile where pᵢ ⊕ s < pᵢ, and reduce it to pᵢ ⊕ s.

    The Sprague-Grundy theorem is why this generalises: every impartial game under the normal play convention is equivalent to a single Nim pile whose size is its Grundy value, and the Grundy value of a sum of games is the XOR of the parts. Nim is not a special case — it is the canonical form.

    #Misère is the interesting variant

    Under misère play (the player taking the last object loses), the strategy is identical until every remaining pile has size 1, at which point it inverts: leave an odd number of single-object piles instead of an even one. Two rules that agree everywhere except at the endgame is exactly the kind of thing that is easier to believe after playing it than after reading it — which is why lab 4 of discrete-mathematics-laboratories ships four variants (classic, misère, single-pile, bounded) rather than one.

    #Difficulty as a probability, not a weaker algorithm

    The AI has one strategy. Difficulty is the probability that it plays the optimal move rather than a random legal one — 15 % / 58 % / 99 %. This is the honest way to build a beatable opponent for a solved game: there is no "weaker" nim engine, only a less consistent one.

    #Structure

    A tkinter GUI, stdlib only. Layered: variants → players → state → screens. Screens are instantiated once and raised with tkraise() rather than destroyed and rebuilt, which keeps widget state alive across navigation.
