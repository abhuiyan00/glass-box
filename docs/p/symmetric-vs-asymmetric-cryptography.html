    Symmetric vs Asymmetric Cryptography

    Two families with different guarantees and wildly different costs. Neither replaces the other — every real protocol uses both, and understanding why is the point of labs 03–06 in cybersecurity-engineering-laboratories.

    #The two problems symmetric cryptography cannot solve

    One shared secret used for both encryption and decryption is fast and simple, and fails twice:

    1. Key distribution. The recipient must already have the key, and getting it to them over the same untrusted channel is the original problem restated.
    2. Key count. Every pair of parties needs its own key, or a third party in the group can read traffic that is not theirs. n participants need n(n−1)/2 keys.

    Asymmetric cryptography dissolves both: each party publishes one key and keeps one, so n participants need n keypairs and the public half can travel over any channel. What it does not give you for free is knowing whose public key you hold — that is a trust problem, not a maths problem, and it is what public-key-infrastructure exists to answer.

    #Why it is slow, and what "slow" means

    Asymmetric schemes rest on one-way functions — problems easy in one direction and hard in reverse. RSA rests on integer factorisation (see rsa-and-integer-factorization); Diffie–Hellman, DSA, ElGamal and the elliptic-curve family rest on discrete logarithms. Both involve modular exponentiation over very large integers, which is orders of magnitude more expensive per byte than a block cipher's round function.

    Lab 04's design is the right way to establish this. Encryption time is measured across two varying factors at once — RSA key length (512, 1024, 2048 bits) against input size (1, 5, 10 MB) — with input diversity held as a third axis carried over from lab 03. Varying both separates what actually drives cost: key length dominates per-operation cost, input size dominates total cost, and input entropy turns out to drive neither. A single-variable experiment would have conflated them.

    The fixtures for that measurement are generated rather than committed — see generate-fixtures-not-commit.

    #The hybrid construction

    The resolution every deployed protocol reaches: encrypt the data with a symmetric cipher, encrypt only the symmetric key with the asymmetric one. One expensive public-key operation on a few hundred bits, then a fast block cipher on the payload. TLS, PGP, S/MIME and encrypted-messaging protocols are all this shape.

    The alternative to shipping a wrapped key is agreeing one: Diffie–Hellman derives a shared secret from public exchanges, so nothing secret crosses the wire at all. Lab 06 attempts DH both as a CrypTool demonstration and practically over OpenSSL between two VMs — and the OpenSSL half is one of the repository's recorded failures ("did not work", "not resolved").     DH's own weakness is stated in the same report and is the reason the two labs that follow exist: unauthenticated key agreement is defeated by a man-in-the-middle-attack, so DH must be signed — which returns to digital-signatures and public-key-infrastructure.

    #Key-length intuition

    Key lengths are not comparable across families. 128-bit symmetric ≈ 3072-bit RSA ≈ 256-bit elliptic curve, roughly, for equivalent work factor. The lab's own answer puts 2048-bit RSA at "generally secure for most purposes" with 3072/4096 for stronger requirements — accurate for 2022, and the sort of claim that needs a date attached, which is why this page carries valid_from.
