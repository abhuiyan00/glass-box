    Deterministic Rules Over a Risk Score

    Decision. health-awareness-support-platform selects an educational route with a pure function over three broad self-ratings. No model, no learned weights, no numeric risk score. The rule set is small enough to read and its output is predictable from its input.

    The student-era design pointed the other way. This is the reversal, and the reasons are recorded in docs/product-decisions.md rather than left as an unbuilt feature.

    #Why not a score

    A number implies a measurement. "Risk: 72" is read as a fact about a person's body, and nothing in the system entitles it to be one — the inputs are three coarse self-reports from a user who may be guessing. Presenting a derived number without the standing to support it is the failure mode here, and it is worse in health than almost anywhere else, because the user cannot easily tell that the confidence is manufactured.

    It changes the regulatory character of the product. Software that scores health status is arguing to be a medical device. Software that routes to source-labelled educational material is not. The decision keeps the product on the side of that line it can actually defend, which is also what makes the disclaimer honest instead of decorative.

    It requires data the product refuses to hold. A useful score wants history, free text, demographics and ideally a diagnosis — precisely the corpus that makes a breach serious. Declining the score is what makes data-minimisation enforceable rather than aspirational: there is no downstream consumer arguing for one more field.

    #What determinism buys

    It is testable. A pure function with three enumerated inputs has an enumerable output space, so learning/tests.py pins the rules exhaustively rather than sampling them. A model would need an evaluation set, a threshold, and a judgement about acceptable error — none of which a portfolio reconstruction can honestly supply.

    It is explainable by construction. The rule that fired is the explanation. No post-hoc attribution, no feature importances that describe the explainer more than the model.

    It cannot drift. Nothing retrains, so behaviour on a given input is fixed for the life of the deployment and any change is a diff. See reproducible-analysis for the same property valued in an analysis pipeline.

    Structurally this is separating-computation-from-io: the deciding part touches no database, no request and no response, which is exactly why it can be a pure function at all.

    #What it costs

    Personalisation, genuinely. The prompt cannot adapt to an individual over time, cannot notice a trend across check-ins, and cannot improve from usage. For a product whose stated purpose is awareness rather than advice, that ceiling is acceptable — and it is a ceiling, not a gap: the function cannot infer a condition, compute a risk, contact a third party, or turn free text into a profile, and those limits are the feature.

    If a future version needed adaptation, the correct move is a different decision made with evidence attached — an evaluation set, a clinical reviewer, a stated error tolerance — not this decision made less carefully.
