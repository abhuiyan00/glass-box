    Man-in-the-Middle Attack

    A class of attack in which the attacker sits between two communicating parties, relaying traffic while both believe they are talking directly to each other. Once positioned, the attacker can read, modify, delay or drop anything that passes. Lab 08 of cybersecurity-engineering-laboratories performs one end to end inside a lab network.

    #The two halves

    Every MitM has a positioning step and an exploitation step, and they are independent problems. Positioning is the hard part; once achieved, exploitation is ordinary traffic handling.

    Positioning techniques the labs enumerate:

    Technique Layer Why it works
    Wi-Fi eavesdropping (rogue AP)linkclients associate to a familiar SSID with no mutual authentication
    ARP poisoninglinkARP has no authentication at all — an unsolicited reply is believed
    DNS spoofing / cache poisoningapplicationname→address answers are accepted without origin authentication
    ICMP redirectsnetworkrouters are instructed to reroute a victim's traffic
    Port stealinglinkthe switch's forwarding table is rewritten so frames arrive at the attacker's port
    STP manglinglinkthe attacker's bridge claims to be the spanning-tree root

    Every one of these is a missing-authentication bug in a protocol designed when the local network was assumed friendly. That is the transferable lesson: the vulnerability is not an implementation defect anywhere, it is an omitted requirement that was reasonable in 1982 and is not now.

    #What the lab demonstrated

    ARP poisoning combined with DNS spoofing against a deliberately vulnerable web application on a Metasploitable VM, with the resulting traffic captured in wireshark — showing that credentials submitted over an unencrypted form are readable in full by the interposed host. The captured material is not reproduced anywhere in this wiki; the finding is that plaintext form submission over a network an attacker can position on offers zero confidentiality, which is the point of the exercise.

    #The cryptographic form of the same attack

    Lab 05 lists MitM among the classical attack models: the attacker terminates two separate encrypted sessions, one with each party, and re-encrypts between them. Both endpoints see a valid session. Unauthenticated Diffie–Hellman is the textbook victim — key agreement without key authentication agrees a key with whoever is on the wire, and the report names this as DH's main risk. See symmetric-vs-asymmetric-cryptography.

    #The defence is authentication, not encryption

    Encryption alone does not help — the attacker simply negotiates with each side separately. What defeats MitM is binding the far end's key to an identity you can verify:

    • public-key-infrastructure and certificate validation for TLS: the chain is checked against a trust store before key agreement completes. Signing the DH exchange is precisely the fix — see digital-signatures.
    • Channel confidentiality below the application, verified by capture. Lab 09 captures a VPN's tunnel interface against the underlying one to confirm what an interposed observer can and cannot read.
    • Link-layer hygiene for the positioning half: dynamic ARP inspection, DHCP snooping, port security, 802.1X. These do not make the protocols authenticated; they make the switch refuse the lie.

    A proxy that intercepts your own browser traffic is the same technique used cooperatively — the lab reports note that owasp-zap and Burp are structurally a MitM where the tester holds both roles. That framing is worth keeping: the mechanism is neutral; only the consent differs.

    #Scope

    Performed in 2022 against lab VMs and deliberately vulnerable targets provided for the purpose. No step-by-step procedure, tool invocation or captured credential from these labs appears in this wiki.
