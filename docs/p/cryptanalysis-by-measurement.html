    Cryptanalysis by Measurement

    Identifying and attacking a cipher by measuring statistical properties of its output rather than by reasoning about its construction. You do not need the algorithm's specification to learn a great deal from a ciphertext — you need a distribution to compare against.

    This is the organising idea of labs 02–05 in cybersecurity-engineering-laboratories, and the one skill in that repository backed by numbers rather than by prose.

    #The five measurements

    Measurement What it exposes
    Entropy (bits/symbol)how close the output is to uniform — a strong cipher's output is near-maximal regardless of its input
    Character frequency histogramwhether the output preserves the source language's letter distribution
    Autocorrelation (coincidence index)text against a shifted copy of itself; peaks at multiples of the key length
    Periodicityrepeated fixed patterns, with an offset and a period
    N-grams (bi/tri/…)block-level structure that single-character frequency misses

    Autocorrelation is the sharpest of the five because it recovers a parameter — the key length — not just a classification. Once the period of a polyalphabetic cipher is known, the problem collapses into that many independent monoalphabetic substitutions, each of which frequency analysis solves.

    #What the measurements actually showed

    The lab-02 entropy figures over one fixed English plaintext are the clearest single result in the repository, because they rank the classical ciphers without any attack being run:

    Cipher Ciphertext entropy (bits/symbol)
    ADFGVX2.26
    Caesar4.15
    Playfair4.47
    Vigenère (short key)4.54
    Vigenère (longer key)4.62
    Hill (2×2 / 5×5)4.63 / 4.68
    Homophonic substitution7.77 (of a maximum 8.00)

    Substitution ciphers barely move entropy at all — they permute symbols without flattening the distribution, which is precisely why frequency analysis defeats them. Homophonic substitution, which maps one plaintext letter onto many ciphertext symbols, nearly reaches maximum entropy and defeats the same tool. Entropy is measuring the defence, not the secrecy.

    Lab 03 repeats the experiment against modern block ciphers with three plaintexts of deliberately different diversity — homogeneous, medium, highly diversified. Under AES and DES in CBC, plaintext entropies of 0.00, 4.04 and 3.97 all produce ciphertext entropy around 7.80–7.83. A modern cipher's output entropy is independent of its input's. That single observation is the boundary between the two halves of the course: the measurements that break classical ciphers stop working exactly here.

    #The limits, recorded honestly

    The lab-05 exercises apply the same instinct to modern algorithms — brute force against AES at 128/192/256 bits, varying how many key bits are unknown and where — and the report states plainly that no exact key was recovered, only a direction. That is the correct outcome and the report does not dress it up. Compare the theory answers in the same labs, several of which are generic filler; the measured work is the part this page cites.

    The classical attack models the labs enumerate — ciphertext-only, known-plaintext, chosen-plaintext, differential, linear, replay, and man-in-the-middle-attack — are the framework these measurements sit inside. Two get their own treatment elsewhere: symmetric-vs-asymmetric-cryptography for why bulk data is not encrypted with a public-key primitive, and rsa-and-integer-factorization for the number-theoretic attacks that replace statistics entirely once the cipher is asymmetric.

    #Scope note

    Every technique here is described as a class of measurement. No ciphertext, key material, or identifier-derived factorisation input from the labs appears in this wiki — see cybersecurity-engineering-laboratories for the redaction rule.
