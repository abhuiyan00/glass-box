    Block Cipher Modes

    A block cipher encrypts one fixed-size block. A mode of operation is the rule for extending it to a message of arbitrary length — and the choice of rule, not the cipher, is where most real-world failures live. AES is not broken; ECB-mode AES is.

    Lab 03 of cybersecurity-engineering-laboratories compares the five standard modes across DES, 3DES and AES.

    #The five modes

    Mode Construction Key property
    ECB — electronic codebookeach block encrypted independentlyidentical plaintext blocks → identical ciphertext blocks. Structure survives encryption
    CBC — cipher block chainingeach block XORed with the previous ciphertext block; an IV starts the chainrandomises repeats; encryption is inherently serial
    CFB — cipher feedbackturns the block cipher into a self-synchronising stream cipherno full block needed before decryption starts
    OFB — output feedbacklike CFB, but feeds back the cipher output rather than the ciphertexta bit error stays in its own block instead of propagating
    CTR — counterencrypts a counter plus a nonce, XORs the result onto the plaintextfully parallelisable and seekable; a repeated (key, counter) pair is catastrophic

    #ECB is the one that matters pedagogically

    ECB fails because it is a deterministic map from block to block. Repeated plaintext blocks produce repeated ciphertext blocks, so the shape of the plaintext leaks — which is why the canonical demonstration is an image that remains recognisable after encryption. This is a cryptanalysis-by-measurement result: frequency analysis, the tool that breaks a simple substitution cipher, applies unchanged to ECB at block granularity. ECB is a substitution cipher whose alphabet is 2^128 symbols wide.

    Every other mode exists to destroy that determinism, by chaining (CBC, CFB), by feeding back independent keystream (OFB), or by never encrypting the plaintext at all (CTR).

    #Error propagation — the property the lab actually measured

    Flip a bit in the ciphertext and see how far the damage spreads on decryption:

    • ECB — damage confined to its own block.
    • CBC — the damaged block, plus the corresponding bit in the next block; recovery afterwards.
    • CFB — propagates forward through subsequent blocks.
    • OFB — confined to the flipped bit; the keystream does not depend on the ciphertext.
    • CTR — confined to the flipped bit, same reason.

    This is a reliability property, not a security one, and confusing the two is a standard error: none of these modes detect the tampering. Bit-flipping in CTR or OFB is a precise modification primitive for an attacker who knows the plaintext layout. Confidentiality without integrity is not enough — which is why authenticated encryption (GCM, CCM, encrypt-then-MAC) is what modern protocols actually deploy. The 2022 lab predates none of that but does not cover it; the modes here are the classical five only.

    #Entropy does not distinguish them

    The lab's own measurements are the useful negative result: three plaintexts of entropy 0.00, 4.04 and 3.97 all yield ciphertext around 7.80–7.83 bits/symbol under both AES-CBC and DES-CBC. Output entropy is therefore not a mode-identification tool, and not a cipher-strength tool either — it tells you only that some modern cipher was used. Identifying the mode requires structural evidence: repeated blocks (ECB), a leading IV, a padding pattern, a ciphertext length that is not a block multiple (stream-like modes).

    #Tooling constraint worth recording

    CrypTool 1 exposes only CBC and ECB; the lab reached OFB and CFB through an online encryption tool. TODO: whether that was a deliberate choice or a gap-filler is unrecorded — see cryptool. Note the practice hazard independently of intent: pushing key material through a third-party web service is exactly what the same course's cia-triad material argues against.
