    RSA and Integer Factorization

    RSA's security rests on one asymmetry: multiplying two large primes is cheap, recovering them from the product is not. Lab 2 of discrete-mathematics-laboratories builds both halves — a working keygen and four attacks against it — and then measures where the attacks stop working.

    #Building the key

    • Miller–Rabin probabilistic primality testing to find p and q.
    • n = p·q, φ(n) = (p−1)(q−1), public exponent e coprime to φ(n).
    • Extended Euclidean algorithm for the private exponent d ≡ e⁻¹ (mod φ(n)).
    • Modular exponentiation (square-and-multiply) for encrypt and decrypt.

    #The four attacks, in order of sophistication

    Attack Idea Where it dies
    Trial divisiontry every candidate divisor up to √nimmediately, for any realistic key size
    Fermat factorizationwrite n = a² − b²; search a upward from ⌈√n⌉fast only when p and q are close
    Pollard's ρcycle detection on a pseudo-random sequence mod nroughly O(n^¼) — a real improvement, still exponential in bit length
    Quadratic sieve (simplified)collect smooth relations, combine into a congruence of squaresthe sub-exponential frontier; the lab's version is a teaching reduction

    #The finding the lab is actually about

    Two measured relationships, plotted rather than asserted:

    1. Runtime and success rate against key size — the curve that turns "RSA is hard" from a claim into an observation.
    2. Sensitivity to |p − q| — Fermat's method collapses the problem when the primes are close together. This is why real keygen enforces a minimum distance between p and q, and it is the most transferable result in the lab: the parameter you would not think to check is the one that breaks the scheme.
