    Network Reconnaissance

    The information-gathering phase that precedes any assessment. Labs 10–12 of cybersecurity-engineering-laboratories walk it in the order it is actually performed: passive collection first, active enumeration second, and only then anything that touches a service.

    #Passive versus active — a real distinction with a real consequence

    Passive collection introduces no traffic to the target. It queries third parties: whois, DNS (dig, host, dnsenum, fierce), search-engine operators, certificate transparency, breach corpora, and the internet-wide scanners (Shodan, Censys, ZoomEye) that already scanned everything. Because the traffic goes to a third party, nothing appears in the target's logs.

    Active enumeration sends packets to the target and gets both better data and a record of your presence. Everything nmap does is active.

    The consequence is a sequencing rule rather than a preference: exhaust passive sources first, because each active probe spends detectability you cannot get back.

    #What passive collection yields

    Hostnames and subdomains, IP ranges and hosting provider, mail and nameserver configuration, DNSSEC presence, exposed documents and their metadata, staff names and email format, technology stack from public fingerprints, and known CVEs for anything identified (via NVD).

    The lab's framing of why the people-oriented half matters is the durable point: OSINT is used to optimise attacks against specific users. Dates, workplaces, schools and relatives feed password guessing and pretexting, because human-chosen secrets correlate with human-knowable facts. The report quotes Kaufman/Perlman/Speciner — "Humans are incapable of securely storing high-quality cryptographic keys" — which is the honest statement of the problem: recon against people is effective because the credential is a person-shaped secret.

    DNS delegation tracing is the clean technical example — walking from the root servers down through each delegation to the target's own nameservers, reading DS and RRSIG records along the way to see where DNSSEC is in force. Entirely public infrastructure, zero contact with the target.

    #What active enumeration adds

    Host discovery, open/closed/filtered port state, service and version identification, OS fingerprinting and network path. nmap recognises six port states, and the three ambiguous ones — filtered, open|filtered, closed|filtered — carry most of the information: they tell you a packet filter is present, which is a finding about the defence rather than about the service.

    Two trade-offs the lab records:

    • Speed against stealth. Timing templates run from paranoid to insane. Results in seconds generate traffic that is trivial to notice; results that are practically undetectable take weeks.
    • Scan type against what you learn. Different TCP flag combinations (connect, SYN, null, FIN, ACK, window) elicit different responses from different stacks and filters — the point of running several is that the differences between their answers characterise the firewall.

    Fragmentation, decoys and timing manipulation are covered as firewall/IDS evasion. Described here as a category only.

    #Recon is the input to everything after it

    Lab 12 makes the pipeline explicit: enumerate services and versions, look up known vulnerabilities for the exact versions found, then attempt exploitation with metasploit against deliberately vulnerable hosts. This wiki records that the chain was attempted and partly failed — the report ends with an exploit that connects but yields nothing — and describes no exploitation step, payload or privilege-escalation procedure. See cybersecurity-engineering-laboratories.

    The defensive reading of the same material is the more useful one: every version string an active scan can read is a version string an attacker can look up. Suppressing service banners, filtering aggressively, and monitoring for scan patterns are all direct responses to what this phase collects.

    #Legality is part of the technique

    The lab material states it plainly: scanning is regulated differently in different jurisdictions, and in some, running a scanner against infrastructure you do not own is an offence regardless of intent. All work here was 2022 coursework against lab targets provided for the purpose.
