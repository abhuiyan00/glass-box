    Wireshark

    A packet capture and protocol analyser: read frames off an interface, dissect them by protocol, and inspect any layer of any packet. Used in labs 08 and 09 of cybersecurity-engineering-laboratories — the only tool in that repository used for both an attack and its verification.

    #The two roles it plays

    As an attack tool. Once positioned between two hosts, capture is how the interposed attacker actually reads the traffic. Lab 08 pairs ARP poisoning with capture against a deliberately vulnerable web application, confirming that credentials submitted over an unencrypted form are fully readable to anyone on the path. See man-in-the-middle-attack. The captured material is not reproduced anywhere in this wiki.

    As a verification tool. Lab 09 captures the VPN tunnel interface (tun0) and the physical interface carrying the encrypted packets, then compares. The tunnel side shows the plaintext the application sees; the physical side shows only ciphertext. That comparison is what turns "the VPN encrypts the traffic" from a claim into an observation.

    The second use is the more valuable one and the more transferable habit: capture is how you check that a security property actually holds rather than trusting the configuration. It is the network equivalent of make-the-formal-object-observable — the invariant is made visible instead of asserted.

    #What the labs read from captures

    • TLS 1.2 versus 1.3 handshakes — which messages appear, what is still in the clear (SNI, certificate in 1.2), and where encryption begins. See public-key-infrastructure.
    • ARP traffic during poisoning: unsolicited replies and the resulting mapping change.
    • DNS query and response pairs during spoofing.
    • Plain protocol traffic against tunnelled traffic on the same conversation.

    #The reading that matters most

    Encryption hides content, not metadata. Even a fully encrypted capture leaks endpoints, timing, packet sizes, and traffic volume — enough to fingerprint what is happening in many cases. Traffic analysis is a separate discipline from cryptanalysis and is not defeated by a stronger cipher. The labs demonstrate the confidentiality half; the metadata half is the caveat this wiki attaches.

    #Practical constraints

    Capture requires either privileged access on the host or a position on the path — a span port, a hub, or the MitM position itself. On a switched network you see only your own traffic and broadcasts, which is exactly why ARP poisoning precedes the capture in lab 08 rather than following it. Filtering is the usability story: a display filter is what makes a multi-gigabyte capture readable, and the skill is knowing what to filter for.
